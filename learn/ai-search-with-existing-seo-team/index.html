Keep the work that already serves buyers

A strong search program may already improve technical access, useful content and conversion paths. Those investments do not become obsolete because buyers use AI assisted search. Google explicitly says established SEO practices remain relevant to its AI features. Any new engagement should begin by recognizing what the current team already owns and does well.

The additional question is whether the business is accurately represented for relevant decisions. A page can perform in conventional search while an AI answer misunderstands the offer, or a brand can be mentioned without a clear reason to choose it. These observations deserve investigation, but they do not automatically justify replacing the existing agency.

Define the gap before defining another retainer

An overlapping scope creates confusion about who approves content, who changes the site and who is accountable when recommendations conflict. A useful division of work follows the actual problem. One team may own technical search while another contributes specialized interpretation of business representation. In another situation, the existing team may be able to handle the whole response with focused guidance.

For example, if a software company is consistently confused with a consulting firm, the product position may need attention. Publishing another set of broad educational articles might not resolve that issue. The first decision is what needs to change, followed by who is best placed to make the change.

Connect interpretation to an agreed owner

Found First provides organic AI visibility work and supporting website and content improvements. Where an established SEO partner is involved, the engagement should make approval and implementation responsibilities explicit. A recommendation should reach the team authorized to act on it, rather than become an instruction from one vendor that another vendor has never agreed to follow.

This relationship also protects useful expertise. Your current team may know historical migrations, content priorities and commercial sensitivities that a new partner cannot infer from a website. Combining that knowledge with a focused AI discovery perspective can support a better decision than treating every existing choice as a defect.

Evaluate progress without competing scorecards

Two agencies can report different things without either report being wrong. Search traffic, observed AI mentions, citations and qualified inquiries measure different aspects of the buyer journey. Problems arise when one metric is presented as proof of another or when each team claims ownership of the same result without evidence.

A good arrangement gives leadership a coherent explanation of what changed and why it matters. Found First can discuss the specific discovery problem, the existing responsibilities and the business outcome you care about. That conversation should establish a useful role for additional help, including the possibility that the immediate priority already belongs with your current team.

Before you decide.

Do we need a separate AI website?+

Usually the first question is how well the existing website explains and supports the offer. A separate site can introduce competing information and ownership. It should have a distinct business reason rather than exist solely because AI search is a new label.

Will both teams need to edit the same content?+

That depends on the agreed responsibilities. Clear review and publication ownership are more important than the number of contributors. The business should avoid simultaneous conflicting changes and make one approved account of each important product or service easy to identify.

How do we know the additional work is different?+

Ask which unresolved buyer or representation problem it addresses, what existing work it relies on and who will implement approved changes. A distinct answer to those questions is more useful than a new acronym attached to familiar recommendations.
