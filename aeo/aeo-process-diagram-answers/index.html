AEO for buyer answers buried in process diagrams

When a diagram contains the only answer to a buying question, publish the deciding condition and its consequence in nearby text. Keep the diagram if seeing the order of work helps buyers understand the rest of the process.

Read the branch labels aloud

Start where a buyer has a choice or an obligation. Follow each arrow to the next action, naming who takes it and what has to be true first. A row of boxes labeled “Review,” “Approval” and “Production” gives no answer to someone asking whether they must sign off. Look to the underlying instructions when the diagram leaves the actor unclear.

Choose a caption or a fuller explanation

A simple fork can often be explained in a caption. Interacting conditions may need a short list of cases that an editor can check against the original. A description such as “flowchart of the approval process” identifies the illustration but gives the buyer no way to make the decision.

Who approves the exhibition text?

Suppose an exhibit fabricator has a proofing diagram and a production checklist. One arrow bypasses “Client review.” The checklist says it applies only to an unchanged reprint from an approved master. Add a sentence under the diagram explaining that exception and stating that revised text requires a new sign-off. If replacing a damaged panel also changes its dimensions, check the fit-approval instructions. The wording can be approved already while the buyer still needs to sign off on size.
