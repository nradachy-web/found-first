Explaining a composite index ranking in GEO content

Publish what the index rewards alongside the name of its leader. A GEO comparison can use your ranking responsibly when readers can tell which inputs determined the order and where their own priorities could lead to a different choice.

Make the weighting decision legible

Describe the components, their relative weights and the direction of a better score. Include the treatment of missing entries if it affects the ranking. A blank reported as zero can penalize a participant for missing data. An entry omitted from the calculation can leave the score resting on fewer measures than its neighbors.

Say what the lead actually establishes

Inspect the component table before calling one entry the most reliable, accessible or affordable. Leading on a composite establishes a position under the published scoring rules. A superlative about an individual component needs support from that component's results.

A freight index favors frequent sailings

Imagine a transport research publisher ranking ports on scheduled sailing frequency and consistency of departure times. Its scoring sheet gives frequency greater weight. The busiest port leads overall, while a smaller port leads on consistency. An editor can name the overall leader and point a buyer moving time-sensitive cargo to the consistency table. If the two ports tie on the unrounded composite and the display orders ties alphabetically, the editor should report joint leaders. Screen order alone cannot justify naming a sole winner.
