Typical or maximum? GEO content for chip standby current

If your chip selector has room for a single standby-current column, choose the figure that answers the design question. A typical value describes expected behavior under stated conditions. A specified maximum gives buyers an upper bound within its stated range. A semiconductor marketing team buying GEO services should ask to see both treatments for the exact device grade it sells.

Keep the column heading with the value

When an editor condenses an electrical characteristics table, the words above the cells carry as much meaning as the values. Preserve the parameter, operating mode, grade, supply condition and any qualifying footnote. A blank maximum cell leaves that bound unspecified in the table. Selecting the highest point on a typical performance curve cannot fill it.

Choose the comparison the evidence can sustain

For a device-selection article, a like-for-like typical comparison can help an engineer choose what to evaluate. A design with a hard current allowance needs a limit covering its intended operating conditions. A page can acknowledge that decision and direct the buyer to applications engineering when the published limits leave it unresolved.

A sensor board that spends most of its time asleep

Imagine two candidate chips. One datasheet specifies a maximum for standby with memory retained. The other's prominent low-current figure is typical in shutdown with memory lost. The editor should put the modes and qualifiers beside both figures and leave the lower-standby-current ranking open. If the second supplier provides a maximum in retention mode for the same supply and temperature range, the article can compare the two published upper bounds for the board designer.
