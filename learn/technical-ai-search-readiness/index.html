A technically reachable page can still be unhelpful

Access is a necessary question, but it is not the whole customer experience. A page can load successfully while explaining the business poorly. Conversely, a strong product explanation is of limited use to discovery if the relevant systems cannot reach the intended public content.

Found First helps separate these concerns. For a software company whose marketing pages are public but whose useful product details sit behind a sign-in, the decision is what information should be publicly available. That is a business and technical choice together. The answer is not automatically to expose private material or rewrite content that already serves customers well.

Understand intentional controls before changing them

A company may restrict crawlers for legitimate reasons. Search access, model training and user-directed retrieval can involve different controls. OpenAI documents independent search and training settings, so a broad instruction to allow or block AI can hide important distinctions.

Found First can discuss the discovery implications with the people who own the site and its policies. The goal is to identify whether a barrier reflects a deliberate business decision or an unintended configuration. We do not treat a marketing objective as permission to remove safeguards, and we do not claim that allowing access guarantees the business will appear in an answer.

Preserve a clear structure for business facts

A visitor should be able to find the product, service or location information that determines fit. If the same offer is scattered across disconnected pages, both the buying experience and the maintenance burden can suffer. Website readiness therefore includes the relationship between the pages, not just whether individual URLs respond.

Our supporting website work connects technical structure with commercial meaning. For a company with several product brands, the site should make the parent relationship and purchasing route understandable. Google says ordinary search foundations remain relevant to its AI features. That supports practical improvements to the existing website rather than a promise of a special AI-only technical shortcut.

Choose the smallest scope that resolves the actual barrier

A readiness concern does not automatically justify a full rebuild. A focused access or architecture issue may be addressable within the current site. In another case, the business may lack the public information needed to explain the offer, making content work the larger priority.

Found First can help marketing and engineering agree on that distinction. A consultation can clarify what should be discoverable, which policies are intentional and what the buyer needs to understand. The resulting scope should state what the website work changes and leave platform inclusion as a separate uncertainty. Technical completion is evidence of readiness work, not proof of a future recommendation.

Before you decide.

Do we need a special AI file or schema?+

Google explicitly says no special AI file or schema is required for its AI search features. Other platforms have their own documentation. A readiness assessment should follow confirmed requirements instead of selling a universal technical shortcut.

Will allowing a search crawler expose private customer information?+

The scope should concern intentionally public content. Private systems and customer data should remain protected. Found First can discuss discovery requirements with your technical team without treating broader access as the default solution.

Can this be done without redesigning the whole site?+

Often the right question is narrower than a redesign. The needed scope depends on the actual barrier and the quality of the public content. Found First assesses that before recommending a larger website project.
